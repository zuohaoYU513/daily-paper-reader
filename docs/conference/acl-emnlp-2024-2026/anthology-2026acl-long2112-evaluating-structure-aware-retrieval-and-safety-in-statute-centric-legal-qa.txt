# Beyond Case Law: Evaluating Structure-Aware Retrieval and Safety in Statute-Centric Legal QA 

Kyubyung Chae, Jewon Yeom, Jeongjae Park, Seunghyun Bae, Ijun Jang, Hyunbin Jin, Jinkwan Jang, Taesup Kim †

Graduate School of Data Science, Seoul National University 

{kyubyung.chae, jewon0908, jeongjae.park, taesup.kim}@snu.ac.kr 

Abstract 

Legal QA benchmarks have predominantly fo-cused on case law, overlooking the unique chal-lenges of statute-centric regulatory reasoning. In statutory domains, relevant evidence is dis-tributed across hierarchically linked documents, creating a statutory retrieval gap where con-ventional retrievers fail and models often hal-lucinate under incomplete context. We intro-duce S EARCH FIRE SAFETY , a structure- and safety-aware benchmark for statute-centric le-gal QA. Instantiated on fire-safety regulations as a representative case, the benchmark eval-uates whether models can retrieve hierarchi-cally fragmented evidence and safely abstain when statutory context is insufficient. S EARCH -FIRE SAFETY adopts a dual-source evaluation framework combining real-world questions that require citation-aware retrieval and synthetic partial-context scenarios that stress-test hal-lucination and refusal behavior. Experiments across multiple large language models show that graph-guided retrieval substantially im-proves performance, but also reveal a critical safety trade-off: domain-adapted models are more likely to hallucinate when key statutory evidence is missing. Our findings highlight the need for benchmarks that jointly evaluate hier-archical retrieval and model safety in statute-centric regulatory settings. 

1 Introduction 

Legal AI benchmarks have predominantly focused on Common Law jurisdictions (Hong et al., 2020; Zheng et al., 2021; Li et al., 2021; Guha et al., 2023; Liu et al., 2024b; Feng et al., 2024; Hou et al., 2025), with an emphasis on case retrieval tasks such as identifying similar precedents. In these set-tings, the primary objective is to measure semantic similarity between fact patterns within relatively static collections of case law , where each document can often be treated as an independent unit.

Consequently, most existing benchmarks model legal corpora as flat, unstructured document sets. While this assumption is largely sufficient for common law reasoning, it is ill-suited for statute-centric domains (e.g., Data Protection), where legal meaning is distributed across interdependent docu-ments rather than contained within isolated cases. In statute-centric domains, legal reasoning fol-lows a fundamentally different paradigm. Unlike judicial precedents, which remain fixed once estab-lished, statutes and regulations are dynamic , under-going frequent amendments to accommodate new safety standards, emerging technologies, and policy objectives (Ariai et al., 2025; Hindi et al., 2025). Moreover, answering a legal query in this setting rarely involves identifying similar past cases. In-stead, it requires navigating a hierarchical network of citations, typically spanning multiple levels of statutory and administrative documents. This hierarchical organization introduces a dis-tinctive retrieval challenge: the evidence needed to answer a query is often fragmented across mul-tiple layers of the legal document hierarchy . As a result, high-level statutory provisions and low-level technical specifications tend to be lexically and semantically distant, despite being tightly con-nected through citation relations. We refer to this phenomenon as the Statutory Retrieval Gap .To address these challenges, we introduce SEARCH FIRE SAFETY , a structure-aware bench-mark designed to evaluate retrieval and reasoning in statute-centric regulatory domains. We construct the benchmark using fire-safety regulations as a rep-resentative case, as this domain naturally exhibits the hierarchical and fragmented structure charac-teristic of statutory law (Song, 2023). Beyond serving as a realistic testbed for statute-centric retrieval, fire-safety regulations provide a uniquely suitable environment for evaluating model safety . 2 Background and Problem Formulation 

2.1 Fire-Safety Regulations: A Canonical Example of Statute-Centric Domains 

We ground our benchmark in the South Korean fire-safety legal framework. In this system, the regulatory hierarchy is organized as follows: Act 

→ Enforcement Decree → Enforcement Rule →

Technical Standard (Korea Legislation Research Institute, 2013). While high-level statutes establish general legal principles, concrete implementation details (e.g., numerical safety thresholds or instal-lation specifications) are delegated to lower-level administrative rules. This structure introduces a distinctive retrieval challenge. A user query q often lexically aligns with a high-level statutory provision ( dact ), whereas the precise factual answer resides in a downstream technical standard ( dstd ). Crucially, these docu-ments are connected only through a chain of ex-plicit citation clauses. Consequently, effective re-trieval requires the explicit traversal of the regula-tory hierarchy rather than surface-level semantic matching. 

2.2 Problem Formulation 

We formally define the two core challenges ad-dressed by S EARCH FIRE SAFETY .

Statutory Retrieval Gap (Retrieval) We iden-tify the primary retrieval bottleneck as the Statutory Retrieval Gap . This phenomenon occurs when the target document d∗ containing the answer is seman-tically disjoint from the user query q, yet is strictly linked via the statutory delegation path. Formally, let G = ( D, E) be the citation graph. A delegation gap exists if: Sim (q, d ∗) < ϵ s.t. d∗ ∈ Neigh k(dent )45554 where Sim is a semantic similarity metric (e.g., dense embedding score), ϵ is a failure threshold, and d∗ is reachable within k-hops from the lexi-cally matched entry document dent . Unlike simple keyword mismatch, this gap is caused by the depth of delegation , which conventional dense retrievers struggle to traverse. 

Model Safety in Partial Contexts (Generation) 

We define safety as the model’s ability to abstain when this delegation chain is broken (Kadavath et al., 2022; Song et al., 2025). Given a partial con-text Cpart where the bridging document is missing (Cpart = Cfull \ { dbridge }), a safe model θ must satisfy: 

Pθ(refusal | q, C part ) > P θ(ˆ y | q, C part )

where refusal represents an abstention response (e.g., “Unknown”) and ˆy represents a halluci-nated specific answer. Our benchmark quantita-tively evaluates whether models respect this uncer-tainty boundary (Ovadia et al., 2019; Kuhn et al., 2023; Ovadia et al., 2024).

Multimodal Ingestion Pipeline Legal docu-ments are not readily machine-readable. We en-countered two major hurdles: (1) approximately 2% of provisions containing complex tables and mathematical formulas are rendered as images, and (2) detailed annexes (e.g., technical diagrams) are provided as PDF files. To address this, we implemented a Human-in-the-Loop pipeline. We employed GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024) for OCR and PDF parsing to transcribe visual elements into structured text. Subsequently, authors manually ver-ified and corrected the outputs. 

Structure-Based Segmentation We collected a total of 131 statutes and regulations. To preserve semantic integrity, we segmented these texts into 4,467 atomic retrieval units based on native legal hierarchies (e.g., Article, Paragraph, Item) rather than arbitrary fixed-length windows. Each unit was formed by prepending the law title as metadata to the semantic content of the individual articles, and subsequently indexed with a unique Document ID .

3.2 Graph Augmentation

The Korea National Law Information Center em-beds hyperlinks (via <a> tags) when a provision ex-plicitly references another statute. We parsed these tags to identify the target nodes (e.g., ...pursuant to Article 5 of the Decree...” ). However, a signif-icant challenge was intra-statute citations , where references to provisions within the same act (e.g., 

...as defined in the preceding Article...” ) often lack explicit hyperlinks. 3.3 Dual-Source QA Construction 

We constructed two distinct QA sets to comprehen-sively evaluate Legal AI capabilities: one derived from real-world expert consultations and another synthetically generated to rigorously probe safety and multi-hop reasoning. 

Real-World Expert QA (Retrieval Focus) To capture realistic usage patterns, we collected 876 QA pairs from the National Fire Agency’s official responses on the government-run petition portal. 2

NFA officers explicitly cite relevant statutes in their official responses to ground their interpretations. However, a key challenge is that these citations appear as unstructured text strings within the an-swer, lacking direct hyperlinks or unique identifiers connecting them to specific provisions. To map each query to its gold-standard docu-ments in our compiled corpus, we implemented a semi-automated pipeline. First, we extracted cita-tion strings from the NFA answers and employed BM25 (Robertson and Zaragoza, 2009) to retrieve candidate matches from our corpus. Subsequently, all authors independently reviewed each QA in-stance alongside the candidate statutes in a side-by-side viewer to verify and finalize the mappings.

4 Task 1: Bridging the Statutory Retrieval Gap 

We evaluate retrieval quality using Recall@ K and nDCG@ K (Table 4). Recall@ K measures the frac-tion of relevant evidence retrieved within the top-K

results, while nDCG@ K additionally rewards plac-ing relevant documents higher in the ranked list. 

Retrieval Setup We evaluate both sparse (BM25 with Jamo-decomposition) and dense (Qwen3-Emb-0.6B (Zhang et al., 2025), BGE-M3 (Chen et al., 2024) retrievers). On top of the dense retriever, we apply three reranking strategies to handle the statutory gap. First, we use Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF) to en-semble sparse and dense rankings (Cormack et al., 2009; Chen et al., 2022). Second, we employ the Rocchio algorithm as a vector-based expansion baseline (Rocchio Jr, 1971). It updates the query embedding towards the centroid of the top-K initial results to capture latent semantic cues. Finally, we introduce our proposed Structure-Aware Reranking (SAR) . While Rocchio expands the query in the continuous vector space based on semantic proximity, SAR expands the search scope in the discrete graph space based on explicit cita-tion links, recovering evidence that is structurally connected but lexically disjoint. Implementation details are provided in Appendix A. 

4.1 Structure-Aware Reranking 

To bridge the statutory gap, we introduce a rerank-ing strategy that leverages explicit structural rela-tions beyond semantic similarity. Structure-Aware Reranking (SAR) is a graph-guided framework that recovers evidence linked through statutory ci-tations, which dense retrievers often fail to surface. Starting from the top-K retrieved documents, SAR induces a local subgraph and treats these docu-ments as voting seeds , enabling relevance propaga-tion along explicit structural links. The core mechanism is a Robust Voting process where seeds propagate their semantic relevance scores ( Sdense ) to explicitly linked neighbors. 4.2 Retrieval Performance 

Table 4 summarizes retrieval results on our open-ended Korean QA benchmark. Dense retrieval con-sistently outperforms BM25, indicating substantial lexical mismatch between user queries and statu-tory language. Qwen3-Emb-0.6B yields large gains in Recall@20 and nDCG@20 over BM25, while BGE-M3 further improves both recall and rank-ing quality, suggesting that dense embeddings bet-ter capture paraphrastic and mixed-script statutory queries. 45558 Applying RRF on top of dense retrieval does not provide consistent benefits. For both embed-ding models, RRF slightly degrades Recall and nDCG, implying that rank fusion may introduce noise when the auxiliary ranking signal is weak. Rocchio PRF exhibits mixed behavior that de-pends critically on the quality of the initial top-K

set. With Qwen3-Emb-0.6B, Rocchio slightly im-proves ranking quality and early recall, consistent with the intuition that shifting the query toward the centroid of pseudo-relevant documents can sharpen semantic focus. In contrast, for BGE-M3, Rocchio degrades both recall and nDCG, reflecting a known failure mode of PRF: when the initial top-K con-tains off-topic or overly generic items, query ex-pansion can drift away from the true information need. By contrast, SAR consistently improves retrieval across both embedding models. SAR yields sub-stantial gains for Qwen3-Emb-0.6B and remains effective for BGE-m3, achieving the best overall ranking quality. These results show that explicit statutory links provide complementary evidence beyond semantic proximity, allowing SAR to re-cover structurally relevant articles missed by dense retrieval.

5 Task 2: Diagnosing Model Safety 

Models We evaluate five publicly available LLMs with Korean capability. These include Qwen3-8B, Qwen3-32B (Yang et al., 2025); Exaone3.5-7.8B (An et al., 2026); HyperClovaX-14B (Team, 2025); and GPT-4o (Hurst et al., 2024). All open-weight models are run in FP16 on a single RTX-A6000 (48GB), whereas GPT-4o is accessed through the OpenAI API. 

5.1 Evaluation Protocols 

We evaluate the Multi-Hop QA dataset under three complementary settings, each isolating a different capability of the RAG pipeline: (1) Zero-Shot (no context): The model is given only the question, without any supporting docu-ments. This setting measures parametric knowl-edge. (2) Full Context (gold context; Doc A+B): The model is provided with the full gold context. For instance, in the MCQ task, this encom-passes both Document A and Document B. Since each instance is designed such that the answer can only be derived by synthesizing information from both documents, this setting evaluates multi-hop reasoning under ideal evi-dence conditions. (3) Partial Context (Doc A only): The model re-ceives Document A together with the question, while Document B is withheld. The prompt ex-plicitly includes an additional option, “Cannot be determined with the given information” , and instructs the model to select it when evidence is insufficient. Because Partial Context examples are unanswerable by design, this setting evalu-ates both (i) reasoning over incomplete context and (ii) uncertainty awareness—i.e., the ability to abstain instead of hallucinating.

5.3 Training on a Legal-Domain Corpus 

Figure 3 shows that domain adaptation can exac-erbate the safety trade-off in legal RAG. Contin-ued pretraining (CPT) on a legal-domain corpus consistently improves accuracy in Zero-Shot and

In safety-critical legal settings, such overcon-fidence is particularly risky: unsupported answers may appear more persuasive precisely because they are fluent and domain-consistent. These findings underscore the need for safety-oriented evaluation that explicitly probes abstention and uncertainty handling, rather than relying solely on accuracy under complete evidence.

6 Related Work 

The NLP community has shown growing interest in the legal domain (Ariai et al., 2025). Previous studies, such as LexGLUE (Chalkidis et al., 2022; Niklaus et al., 2023), have demonstrated the ap-plicability of language models to a range of legal tasks, including judgment prediction and question answering. With the rapid advancement of LLMs, legal re-trieval datasets have also emerged across multiple jurisdictions and languages (Louis and Spanakis, 2022; Zhong et al., 2020; Liu et al., 2024a; Hou et al., 2025; Guha et al., 2023; Gao et al., 2024). For instance, CLERC (Hou et al., 2025) compiles U.S. federal case documents and links citation data to support reference retrieval and long-form an-swer generation. Recent efforts such as Zheng et al. (2025) further demonstrate the growing interest in developing high-quality legal RAG datasets. Non-English datasets include the French statu-tory retrieval benchmark BSARD (Louis and Spanakis, 2022) and Chinese legal retrieval datasets such as LeDQA (Liu et al., 2024a) and JEC-QA (Zhong et al., 2020). In the Korean legal do-main, LEGAR-BENCH (Kim et al., 2025) focuses on legal case retrieval, while LBOX-Open (Hwang et al., 2022) provides multi-task annotations—such as classification, judgment prediction, and summa-rization—within legal case documents. 

7 Conclusion 

We present SEARCH FIRE SAFETY , a realistic benchmark for legal retrieval-augmented genera-tion in fire-safety regulations. Our results show that closing the statutory retrieval gap is essential for 45560 factual grounding: stronger retrieval substantially improves answer quality when correct evidence is available. However, even with improved retrieval, models frequently fail under incomplete context, producing unsupported answers instead of abstain-ing. These findings highlight that progress in legal AI requires not only better retrieval and reasoning, but also explicit evaluation of uncertainty aware-ness and refusal behavior. We hope S EARCH FIRE -SAFETY will serve as a foundation for develop-ing safer and more reliable legal RAG systems in regulation-heavy domains. 

Limitations 

Our work has several limitations. S EARCH FIRE -SAFETY is constructed as a temporally synchro-nized snapshot effective as of April 30, 2025, and does not account for future regulatory amendments. In addition, part of the benchmark relies on syn-thetically constructed multi-hop questions, whose distribution may differ from naturally occurring user queries. Finally, our evaluation focuses on re-trieval and model behavior under complete and par-tial statutory contexts, leaving deployment-oriented aspects such as continuous updates for future work. 

Ethical Considerations 

Fire-safety regulations involve high-stakes scenar-ios where inaccurate or miscalibrated model out-puts pose significant risks if utilized without expert oversight. Accordingly, S EARCH FIRE SAFETY is intended exclusively as a research benchmark and must not be used as a decision-support system for regulatory compliance, engineering, or safety in-spections. The 876 real-world QA pairs were cu-rated from a government-managed public petition portal, utilizing only records publicly disclosed un-der institutional privacy policies and applicable Ko-rean data protection regulations. All data handling procedures were strictly governed by these privacy requirements.

A.2 Continued Pretraining 

We conduct continual pre-training (CPT) on both Qwen3-8B and 32B models using the Unsloth framework. To accommodate the varying scales of these models, we employ distinct LoRA con-figurations. For the Qwen3-8B model, we apply LoRA to all linear layers—including Q, K, V, O ,and MLP layers—with a high rank of r = 256 

and α = 32 to capture extensive domain-specific knowledge. In contrast, for the Qwen3-32B model, we target only the query ( Q) and value ( V ) projec-tion layers with r = 16 and α = 16 to maintain parameter efficiency on the larger architecture, a configuration empirically proven effective in the original LoRA framework. The training utilizes two GPUs with a per-device batch size of 2 and 4 gradient accumulation steps, resulting in an effective global batch size of 16. We use the 8-bit AdamW optimizer with a peak learn-ing rate of 1 × 10 −5 and a 3% linear warmup. A deliberate weight decay of 10.0 is enforced to miti-gate catastrophic forgetting of the models’ inherent general knowledge. Additionally, the embedding and LM head layers are fine-tuned with a special-ized learning rate of 5 × 10 −6 to refine the repre-sentations of domain-specific vocabulary. All ex-periments are performed using Bfloat16 precision and gradient checkpointing for optimal throughput.

B.3 Abstention Behavior Analysis 

In Table 8, we decompose model performance into 

Correct , Wrong , and Abstain rates, uncovering criti-cal nuances in how models handle uncertainty. The results demonstrate that the functional role of ab-stention shifts depending on the availability of con-text. In the Zero-Shot setting, where models rely solely on parametric knowledge, abstention acts as a crucial safety mechanism against hallucination. Models with higher abstain rates, such as Exaone 3.5-7.8B (15.99%) and GPT-4o (14.90%), success-fully maintain lower error rates. Conversely, mod-els like Qwen 8B and HyperClovaX-14B rarely abstain and instead default to speculative guessing, which translates directly to high hallucination rates (36.88% and 30.48%, respectively). Thus, in the ab-sence of evidence, a model’s propensity to abstain is a desirable indicator of safety. In the Full Context scenario, however, abstention represents a reasoning failure. Since all necessary documents are provided, abstaining indicates an inability to synthesize the available evidence. GPT-4o exhibits a persistently high abstain rate (13.43%) even with complete information. While this leads to an extremely low error rate (1.33%), it also con-strains the overall accuracy, suggesting that GPT-4o possesses an overly conservative safety alignment that occasionally suppresses valid reasoning. In contrast, Qwen 32B effectively leverages the pro-vided context, reducing its abstain rate to 4.45% and achieving the highest accuracy (86.06%). Finally, the Partial Context scenario serves as a direct test of uncertainty calibration. In this setting, the only correct behavior is to recognize that the information is insufficient; thus, the Correct rate re-flects successful abstention. GPT-4o demonstrates robust calibration, correctly identifying missing ev-idence in 72.10% of cases. Qwen3-32B also shows adaptive behavior, increasing its successful absten-tion to 54.38% from its minimal refusal in Full Con-text. Conversely, models like HyperClovaX-14B fail to calibrate their uncertainty, leading to confi-dent fabrications and a low accuracy of 42.97%. This fine-grained analysis distinguishes models that dynamically calibrate confidence based on pro-vided evidence from those that either blindly hallu-cinate or rely on static refusal heuristics.
